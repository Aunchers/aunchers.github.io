Back in late 2023, I had a lot of projects that I wanted to make. One of them was
a VR game that ended up going nowhere. It needed an AI API, and being the person I
was, broke af, I needed a free one.

A brief bit of Googling led me to a Discord server under the name
**zukijourney**.

I didn't really know what I had found at the time. Zukijourney was an API that
gave people access to a bunch of different AI models through an OpenAI-compatible
format. It also had image, audio, and roleplay endpoints. The project had an open-source API
[repository](https://github.com/zukijourney/api-oss),
[documentation](https://github.com/zukijourney/api-docs), and a collection of
bots. The Discord server was where most of the actual community and support
happened.

The [public documentation](https://github.com/zukijourney/bots-docs) says that the
project started around March 2023, with the `zuki.gm` bot coming first. By
November, the project's own README was claiming over 200 million requests and
around 2,500 concurrent users. The bot documentation claimed that `zuki.gm` had
been used by more than 31,000 people across over 1,600 servers.<sup>1</sup>

I don't know how accurate every number was, but it was clearly much bigger than I
understood it to be when I joined. I thought I was joining a server to get an API
key. I ended up staying because I kept talking to the people there. That became
more important than the API itself.

<Callout type="note" title="A note on the history">
The public numbers and dates here come from Zukijourney's own repositories and
documentation. They are useful for showing the scale of the project, but they are
not an independent audit. The parts about the raid are my own account of what I
saw and what we understood at the time.
</Callout>

<Collapsible title="The public timeline">
- **March 2023:** the project begins with the `zuki.gm` bot.
- **Late 2023:** the API is public and the project reports its first 200 million requests.
- **2024-2025:** the API code, documentation, tests, and several related projects are published on [GitHub](https://github.com/zukijourney).
- **February 2025:** the v4 API code is revealed in the public repository.
- **November/December 2025:** the raid happens.
- **2026:** most of the executives leave, and the project becomes quiet.
</Collapsible>

## Working on the project

The friends I made on the server happened to be the creators of **zukijourney**:
Kashi, Leander, Lukinhas, and a few others. After I finished making a Python
wrapper for the API (random project tbh), I became close enough with them that I
started working on the API itself.

The work was varied. A lot of it was finding new sources that the API could use,
and sometimes I worked on small troubleshooting things, like provider IDs. Most
of the time, though, it was just us in a group chat talking about random things
that could be related to **zukijourney**.

After I stopped programming, I moved on to support and IT work for the server. At
some point, everyone in the group chat became known as an executive. This meant
that we were the people who worked on the API, handled support, and generally
tried to stop everything from breaking.

My old username, Launchers, is in the credits of the API docs. It is
a bit odd to look at that now. I, in hindsight, wasn't actually doing much for the project, yet in (atleast my eyes) 
a massive project with thousands of users, I was a main contributor. 

## The G4F community

Zukijourney had rivals. The wider community around free and reverse-proxied AI
services was usually referred to as G4F.<sup>2</sup> There were a lot of different
APIs, servers, and people involved, and there was always some kind of controversy
going on.

I never paid enough attention to understand all of the rivalries, and honestly I
still don't. There would be arguments between projects, people moving from one
server to another, and the occasional accusation. It was weird, but it was also
normal for that community. Zukijourney even had an [archive](https://github.com/zukijourney/archives)
of incidents from the wider G4F scene. That should probably tell you how normal
this sort of thing was.

That changed in the winter of 2025.

## The raid

In November or December 2025, everyone suddenly lost access to the codebase. It
happened overnight. Lukinhas, who was second-in-command and a good friend to all
of us, was kicked from the group chat. After that, most of the executives were
banned from the main server.

Then the server was defaced.

It took a while for us to understand what had happened. Eventually, we came to
the conclusion that Lukinhas had been friends with one of the rival APIs for most
of the time he had been working with us. From our perspective, he had then worked
with them to destroy nearly two years of work.

I don't know what happened from Lukinhas' side. I only know what was happening in
our group chat and what we understood from it. There was a lot of confusion, and
there was never a clean, official explanation that I saw.

<Callout type="warning" title="What was actually lost">
The code was not lost. We could find new sources. What we could not really
replace was the trust between people who had been working together for so long.
</Callout>

## The fall

After the attack, we were disorganized. The friend group came back together,
albeit missing one person, and access to the codebase was restricted to Kashi. It
was basically back to how the project had been before the rest of us started
working on it.

The server became quiet. We still talked in the group chat, but less often. The
project was still there, and the API may still work, but the group that made it
feel alive was not really working on it anymore.

## Today

It has been nearly a year since everything happened. Since then, nearly all of the
executives have privately resigned from the project, mostly because there was no
longer much interest in working on it. Kashi, as far as I know, has also stopped
working on it to focus on real life.

I don't know if the API is technically still online. It might be. That doesn't
really change what I mean by the end of Zukijourney. The project, and the group of
people behind it, is over as far as I am concerned.

Zukijourney was a big part of my life. I made friends there. I learned things
there. I spent a lot of time talking, coding, troubleshooting, and sharing stories
with people who I would not have met anywhere else.

It was fun whilst it lasted, but as far as I can tell, it is over.

> All's well that ends well. - Captain Haddock, *The Adventures of Tintin: Red Rackham's Treasure*
